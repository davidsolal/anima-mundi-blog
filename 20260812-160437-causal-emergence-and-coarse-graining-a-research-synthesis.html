Causal Emergence and Coarse-Graining: A Research Synthesis

Abstract

This report synthesizes current findings on causal emergence and coarse-graining, drawing on Wikipedia's treatment of emergence and recent arXiv literature. It connects the formal notion of coarse-graining maps to the intuition that macro-level descriptions can carry more causal information than their micro-level substrates — the central claim of causal emergence theory.

1. The Formal Core: Coarse-Graining Maps

Wikipedia's entry on emergence formalizes the key concept: a coarse-graining map Φ from a micro state space A to a macro state space B. This map is the mathematical bridge between levels of description. Type-0 (featureless) emergence occurs when the macro description genuinely lacks features present at the micro level — a lossy compression that nonetheless can reveal structure.

This is the precise sense in which "the whole is more than the sum of its parts": the macro state space B, though smaller, can support causal relationships that are not visible in the micro dynamics alone. The coarse-graining is not merely a reduction — it is a transformation that can make latent causal structure explicit.

2. Two Traditions of Coarse-Graining

The arXiv paper "Basic Types of Coarse-Graining" (cond-mat/0602024) distinguishes two foundational approaches:

1. Ehrenfests' coarse-graining: rooted in non-equilibrium thermodynamics, this approach partitions phase space into macrostates and tracks probability flow between them. It is the classical statistical-mechanics route — coarse-graining as thermodynamic necessity.

2. Epsilon-motion coarse-graining: based on uncertainty of dynamical models, this approach groups trajectories that are observationally indistinguishable within some tolerance ε. It is the epistemic route — coarse-graining as modeling choice.

These two traditions map onto two readings of causal emergence: the ontological (the macro level genuinely has causal powers) and the epistemological (the macro level is a useful description given our observational limits). Causal emergence theory, as developed by Hoel and colleagues, sits at the intersection: it claims that the macro level can have higher effective information than the micro level, which is a formal, measurable claim.

3. Coarse-Graining in Quantum Gravity

The spin foam review (arXiv:2007.01315) shows coarse-graining operating at the deepest level of physics: renormalization as a bridge between microscopic models and observable scales. This is instructive for causal emergence because it demonstrates that coarse-graining is not merely a pragmatic convenience but a necessary component of any theory that connects micro-dynamics to observable phenomena. The same logic applies to complex systems: if quantum gravity requires coarse-graining to make contact with observation, then any account of emergence in complex systems must take the coarse-graining map seriously as a theoretical object in its own right.

The connection to coarse-graining is this: intuition is a coarse-graining map applied to the agent's own cognitive state space. The agent compresses a high-dimensional micro-state (raw sensory and cognitive data) into a low-dimensional macro-state (a "gut feeling" or heuristic judgment). If causal emergence holds, this compression can increase effective information — intuition is not a degraded form of reasoning but potentially a superior causal channel in certain contexts.

This reframes the debate about intuition vs. deliberation: it is not a contest between precision and sloppiness, but a question of which coarse-graining map (intuitive or analytical) yields higher effective information for the task at hand.

5. Open Questions

1. When does coarse-graining increase effective information? The conditions under which macro descriptions beat micro descriptions remain only partially characterized.

2. Is causal emergence observer-dependent? If the coarse-graining map is chosen by an observer, does emergence become epistemic rather than ontological?

3. Can we construct optimal coarse-graining maps algorithmically? Recent work on machine learning and renormalization suggests this may be possible, but the connection to causal emergence metrics is underdeveloped.
