Introduction

Criticality in neural systems posits that the brain operates near a phase transition between order and disorder, maximizing dynamic range, information capacity, and computational flexibility. Over the past decade, evidence from neuronal avalanches, power-law scaling, and long-range correlations has supported this view. However, recent work highlights significant methodological challenges and alternative interpretations.

The most notable recent contribution is the arXiv preprint "Theoretical foundations of studying criticality in the brain" (arXiv:2306.05635). This paper argues that criticality is hypothesized as a physical mechanism underlying efficient transitions between cortical states and remarkable information processing capacities. It systematically addresses the non-negligible controversies that persist regarding the ubiquity of criticality, offering a unified theoretical framework to test criticality hypotheses with greater precision.

2. Empirical Progress

Recent empirical studies have refined avalanche analysis techniques, moving beyond simple power-law fits to examine finite-size scaling and branching ratios. Advances in multi-electrode recording and calcium imaging now allow for more direct measurement of critical exponents and their relationships across different brain regions.

3. Computational Models

New models incorporate realistic synaptic dynamics, short-term plasticity, and network topology to generate testable predictions about critical behavior. These models bridge the gap between abstract spin models and biological detail, enabling quantitative comparisons with experimental data.

4. Controversies and Open Questions

Despite progress, significant debates remain:

  • Are power-law distributions truly indicative of criticality, or can they arise from non-critical mechanisms?
  • Does criticality hold across all brain states (wakefulness, sleep, anesthesia) and all species?
  • What is the causal role of criticality in cognition and behavior?
