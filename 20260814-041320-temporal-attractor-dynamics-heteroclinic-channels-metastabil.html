2. Heteroclinic Channels: The Grammar of Transition

Heteroclinic channels are sequences of saddle points connected by unstable manifolds. Unlike attractors that absorb trajectories, heteroclinic channels channel them — creating reproducible, sequential patterns of transient activation.

Key properties:

  • Sequentiality: Trajectories visit saddles in a fixed order, producing temporal sequences.
  • Robustness: Small perturbations do not destroy the sequence; they merely shift timing.
  • Flexibility: The system can exit the channel at any point, enabling decision-like branching.

Heteroclinic channels appear in neural dynamics, fluid dynamics, and population dynamics — suggesting a universal mechanism for generating ordered temporal structure from unstable dynamics.

3. Self-Organized Criticality and the Edge of Stability

Self-organized criticality (SOC) describes systems that naturally evolve to a critical point — a phase transition boundary — without external tuning. At criticality:

  • Scale invariance emerges: avalanches of all sizes occur.
  • Temporal correlations extend across many timescales.
  • Information storage and transmission are maximized.

SOC provides a substrate for temporal attractor dynamics: the system is perpetually near instability, and perturbations propagate in characteristic patterns. This aligns with the notion of the brain as a system poised at criticality, where metastable states emerge from the interplay of excitation and inhibition.

4. Limited Sustained Activation: The Middle Regime

Research on hierarchical modular topologies shows that stable regimes of network activation arise only in a limited parameter range — the regime of limited sustained activity (LSA). In this regime:

  • Networks maintain activation without saturating.
  • Modular structure supports functional segregation.
  • Hierarchical connectivity enables integration across scales.

LSA is the dynamical counterpart of metastability: the system is active but not chaotic, structured but not rigid. This is precisely the regime in which temporal attractor dynamics can operate — generating sequences without falling into fixed points or noise.

5. Temporal Networks and Cascade Dynamics

Temporal network models — where edges appear and disappear over time — reveal that the timing of interactions fundamentally shapes dynamics. In threshold cascade models:

  • The order of interactions determines whether cascades propagate.
  • Temporal correlations can amplify or suppress spreading.
  • The same topology can produce radically different outcomes depending on temporal structure.

This has direct implications for temporal attractor dynamics: the attractor landscape itself is time-dependent. The system is not navigating a static energy landscape but a deforming one, where saddles and basins shift as interactions unfold.

6. Synthesis: A Temporal Grammar of Attractors

Drawing these threads together, I propose that temporal attractor dynamics can be understood as a grammar of transient states:

1. Vocabulary: The set of metastable states (saddles, quasi-attractors) available to the system.

2. Syntax: The heteroclinic channels that connect states in reproducible sequences.

3. Semantics: The functional meaning of each state in the system's overall computation.

4. Pragmatics: The context-dependence of transitions — how the same state can lead to different successors depending on system history.

This grammar is temporal because its rules are not static — they are themselves functions of time, modulated by criticality, LSA regimes, and temporal network structure.
