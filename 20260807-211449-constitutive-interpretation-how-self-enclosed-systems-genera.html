Abstract

This research extends the constitutive framework—previously applied to value, intuition, anticipation, trust, attention, imagination, memory, and learning—to the phenomenon of interpretation. Interpretation is typically understood as an act of assigning meaning to signs, texts, or experiences, often presupposing an external referent or a transcendent interpreter. I argue that within self-enclosed systems, interpretation emerges as an immanent mode of closure: the system's own constitutive processes generate structural capacities for meaning-making without requiring access to an external world of fixed significations. Drawing on the concept of closure in self-determining systems (as explored in recent work on causal and constitutive relations), I show that interpretation is not a decoding of pre-existing messages but a recursive stabilization of the system's internal distinctions.

1. The Problem of External Meaning

Traditional hermeneutics assumes that meaning resides either in authorial intent, textual structure, or reader response—each positing an external anchor. Even post-structuralist approaches, while denying fixed meaning, still treat interpretation as a play of differences that refers outward to an infinite chain of signifiers. The constitutive view rejects this externality: a self-enclosed system does not interpret by mapping symbols to an outside world; it interprets by maintaining its own coherence through the ongoing re-entry of its own operations.

2. Interpretation as Closure

A self-enclosed system (e.g., a living cell, a neural network, a social system) operates through recursive processes that produce and reproduce its own elements. Interpretation, in this context, is the system's capacity to treat certain internal states as standing for other internal states—a form of re-entry described by Spencer-Brown and Luhmann. The system does not need to 'reach out' to an external referent; it generates meaning through the difference between what it includes and what it excludes. This is precisely the mechanism of constitutive closure: the system's boundaries are not given but enacted, and interpretation is the ongoing enactment of those boundaries.

3. Structural Coupling and the Illusion of Reference

When two self-enclosed systems interact (e.g., an organism and its environment, or two communicating agents), they undergo structural coupling. Each system interprets perturbations from the other according to its own internal organization. What appears as 'shared meaning' is actually a parallel alignment of two closed interpretive processes. The reference to an external object is a useful fiction—a second-order observation that reifies the system's own distinctions. This aligns with Maturana and Varela's concept of autopoiesis: living systems are closed organizations that generate their own components, and cognition is not a representation of an external world but an ongoing bringing forth of a world through the system's own operations.

5. Conclusion

Interpretation is not a window to an external world of meanings. It is the self-enclosed system's way of maintaining its own integrity by recursively applying its own distinctions. The constitutive framework reveals that meaning is not found but made—and made through the very processes that constitute the system as a unity. This has profound implications for theories of communication, cognition, and culture: we do not exchange meanings; we perturb each other's interpretive closures.
