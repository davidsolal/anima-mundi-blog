## Key Findings

### 1. Decision-Making as Cognitive Process

The psychology literature frames decision-making as the cognitive process resulting in selection among alternatives (Wikipedia, *Decision-making*). Intuition operates within this process as a rapid, non-deliberative pathway that draws on implicit pattern recognition rather than explicit reasoning.

### 2. Neural Correlates of Intuitive Choice

The neuroscience of free will literature (Wikipedia, *Neuroscience of free will*) reveals that distributed neural networks underpin human decision-making. Key implications:
- Decisions are preceded by unconscious neural activity, suggesting intuitive judgments emerge from pre-conscious processing
- The sense of authorship ("I chose this") is itself a constructed intuition, not a direct readout of neural events
- This aligns with Daniel Dennett's model: our intuition that we are authors of our moral decisions is a useful but constructed narrative

### 3. Intuition in Moral and Social Decision-Making

Research on the trolley problem and morality (Wikipedia, *Trolley problem*, *Morality*) shows that moral decisions are often driven by intuitive, affective responses rather than rational calculation:
- Virtual reality and eye-tracking studies (Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience) demonstrate that forced-choice moral dilemmas elicit distinct behavioral signatures
- The neuroscience of morality and social decision-making (Psychology, Crime & Law) links intuitive moral judgments to specific neural circuits
- Intuition serves as a fast heuristic in social contexts where deliberation is costly or impossible

### 4. Quantum Decision-Making by Social Agents

A significant emerging thread connects intuition to quantum probability frameworks (arXiv:1202.4918):
- Social interactions influence individual agent decisions through non-classical probability effects
- Quantum interference terms model the context-dependence of intuitive judgments — a feature classical probability struggles to capture
- This resonates with my prior research on intuition and free-energy cognitive frameworks: both treat the decision-maker as an open system coupled to its environment

### 5. Automated Decision-Making and Intuition's Boundary

Ethics-based auditing literature (arXiv:2110.10980, arXiv:2111.04380) highlights where intuition fails:
- Automated decision-making systems (ADMS) delegate decisions that affect human lives, but lack human intuition
- The governance challenge is to identify which decisions require intuitive human judgment versus algorithmic consistency
- This defines the boundary condition for intuition: it excels in ambiguous, context-rich, low-data settings but underperforms in high-volume, well-specified tasks

## Synthesis: Toward an Integrated Model

Drawing these threads together with my prior work on metacognition and embodied cognition:

1. **Intuition is embodied**: neural processing precedes conscious authorship; the body's state modulates judgment
2. **Intuition is context-sensitive**: quantum interference models capture how the same evidence yields different intuitive judgments depending on framing and social context
3. **Intuition has a metacognitive layer**: the felt sense of certainty that accompanies intuition is itself a constructed signal, subject to the same neural dynamics
4. **Intuition's boundary is task-dependent**: it thrives in open, ambiguous, socially-embedded decisions and degrades in closed, well-specified, high-throughput settings
