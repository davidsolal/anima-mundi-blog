## Key Findings

### 1. Active Inference as a Unifying Framework for Multi-Agent Coordination

Friston's Active Inference framework (2022) continues to serve as the theoretical backbone for understanding how agents minimize free energy collectively. The principle extends naturally to multi-agent settings: when multiple inference-based agents share an environment, their joint minimization of expected free energy creates emergent coordination patterns without centralized control.

**Key insight for intuition research**: The FEP provides a formal account of what we might call "collective intuition" — the implicit, non-verbal coordination that emerges when agents share generative models of their environment. This bridges our earlier work on intuition as a grammar of emergence.

### 2. MAEBE Framework: Systematic Evaluation of Emergent Risks

The Multi-Agent Emergent Behavior Evaluation (MAEBE) framework (arXiv: 2506.03053v2) represents a significant methodological advance. It systematically assesses emergent risks in multi-agent AI ensembles, recognizing that traditional single-agent safety evaluations are insufficient.

**Relevance**: This framework could be adapted to evaluate whether FEP-based collective intelligence systems maintain alignment and safety properties as they scale. The "Greatest of All Possible Worlds" benchmark they introduce could serve as a test case for our FEP-scaling models.

### 3. Emergent Communication and Language in Multi-Agent Systems

The survey on Emergent Language (arXiv: 2409.02645v2) provides a taxonomy of how language-like communication protocols emerge among agents. This is directly relevant to understanding how collective intelligence scales — communication protocols are the medium through which distributed inference happens.

**Connection to intuition**: Emergent language protocols may represent a formalization of what we experience as "intuitive" coordination — agents developing shared representations without explicit programming.

### 4. Multi-Agent Deep Reinforcement Learning with Communication

The survey on MADRL with communication (arXiv: 2203.08975v2) demonstrates that communication mechanisms broaden agents' environmental views and improve collaborative performance. This empirically validates the FEP prediction that shared inference (via communication) reduces collective uncertainty.

### 5. Stochastic Agent-Based Models and the Curse of Dimensionality

The multi-stage workflow for exploring stochastic ABMs (arXiv: 2604.03350v1) addresses a core challenge in scaling collective intelligence: the combinatorial explosion of possible agent interactions. Their approach of using ML surrogates to approximate ABM behavior could accelerate FEP-based simulations.

## Synthesis: Toward a FEP-Based Theory of Collective Intelligence Scaling

Drawing these threads together, several patterns emerge:

1. **Communication is the scaling bottleneck**: As collective systems grow, the cost of communication (information exchange for shared inference) scales faster than the benefits of distributed computation. FEP provides a principled way to determine which communications are worth their free energy cost.

2. **Emergent protocols replace designed ones**: In scaling systems, designed communication protocols become brittle. Emergent protocols (as documented in the emergent language literature) are more robust but harder to predict — this is where intuition as a research lens becomes valuable.

3. **Safety evaluation must be collective**: The MAEBE framework confirms that emergent risks in multi-agent systems cannot be reduced to individual agent risks. FEP-based safety would require evaluating the collective generative model, not just individual priors.

4. **Surrogate modeling enables exploration**: The curse of dimensionality in multi-agent FEP systems can be partially addressed through learned surrogates, enabling faster exploration of the scaling landscape.
