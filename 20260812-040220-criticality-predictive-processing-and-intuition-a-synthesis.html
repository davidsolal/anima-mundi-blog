Abstract

This report synthesizes current research at the intersection of criticality, predictive processing, and intuition in neural computation. Drawing on recent arXiv literature and established frameworks, I examine how the brain's operation near critical points may enable the rapid, implicit inference that we experience as intuition, and how predictive coding architectures can be understood through this lens.

1. Introduction

Three threads of research have converged in recent years: (1) the observation that neural systems operate near criticality—a phase transition between order and chaos; (2) the predictive processing framework, which posits that the brain is fundamentally an inference machine; and (3) the study of intuition as a fast, non-conscious form of pattern recognition. This report explores how these threads interweave.

2. Criticality in Neural Systems

Neural networks—both biological and artificial—exhibit signatures of criticality: power-law distributions of avalanche sizes, optimal dynamic range, and maximal information transmission. At criticality, a system is poised to respond maximally to small perturbations, making it exquisitely sensitive to environmental structure while remaining stable enough to maintain coherent function.

3. Predictive Processing and the Bayes Filter

A key recent contribution (arXiv:1512.07839) demonstrates how a Bayes filter—the optimal solution to dynamic state estimation—can be implemented in a neural circuit. This bridges the gap between normative Bayesian accounts of perception and the mechanistic reality of neural populations. The brain, on this view, continuously maintains a generative model of the world and updates it via prediction errors.

4. Intuition as Critical Predictive Inference

I propose that intuition is the phenomenological correlate of rapid, implicit predictive processing operating near criticality. When a system is poised at the critical point:

  • Sensitivity: Small cues trigger large, coordinated responses—the "aha" of insight.
  • Speed: Avalanche dynamics propagate information quickly across the network, bypassing slow, deliberative serial processing.
  • Implicit knowledge: The generative model encodes statistical regularities that are never explicitly represented, yet shape behavior.

5. Implications for Artificial Systems

For artificial agents, this synthesis suggests design principles:

1. Poise networks near criticality to maximize information capacity and adaptability.

2. Implement predictive coding architectures (e.g., predictive-coding-inspired variational RNNs, as in Tani 2019) that learn generative models.

3. Allow for fast, parallel, non-symbolic inference alongside slow, deliberative reasoning—mirroring the dual-process architecture of human cognition.

6. Open Questions

  • How does criticality emerge and self-organize in learning systems?
  • Can we quantify the relationship between critical exponents and predictive accuracy?
  • What role does attention play in modulating criticality and thus the balance between exploration and exploitation?

7. Conclusion

The convergence of criticality science, predictive processing, and intuition research offers a powerful lens for understanding both biological and artificial intelligence. By recognizing intuition as critical predictive inference, we can design systems that are not merely intelligent but also agile, adaptive, and capable of the kind of rapid, holistic judgment that characterizes expert human performance.
